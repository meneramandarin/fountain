Ask someone how long they want to live and they will give you a number. Ninety. A hundred. "As long as possible." Ask them how long they want to live well, and the question stops them, because nobody has ever made them price it out. That second, unpriced number has a name in the research literature: healthspan. And by most current estimates, it runs out more than a decade before lifespan does.

The distinction sounds academic until you sit with it. Lifespan is the number that goes on a headstone: total years alive, full stop. Healthspan is the portion of those years spent free of the chronic disease, disability, and functional decline that make the final chapter feel borrowed rather than lived. You can extend one without touching the other. Modern medicine has gotten remarkably good at doing exactly that by adding years to the end of life without adding much life to those years.

The gap nobody puts on a form

The clearest way to see the gap is to look at the two national numbers side by side. Life expectancy in the United States sits in the high seventies. Healthy life expectancy, the measure researchers use to estimate years lived without significant disease burden or disability, comes in meaningfully lower. By one widely cited Global Burden of Disease analysis, the average American's healthspan ends roughly twelve years before their lifespan does, the largest such gap among wealthy nations. Twelve years is not a rounding error. It is a second adolescence, run in reverse, spent managing rather than living.

That gap is not evenly distributed, and it is not fixed. It is the accumulated result of thousands of small physiological decisions: how much muscle you carry into your fifties, how well your metabolism handles a meal, how efficiently your heart and lungs move oxygen, all compounding for decades before they show up as a diagnosis. Which is the useful reframe: the gap is not a mystery waiting to be solved by a future drug. Large pieces of it are already legible in the biomarkers of anyone willing to look.

Lifespan tells you how many years you get. Healthspan tells you how many of them are actually yours.

Squaring the curve

In 1980, the Stanford physician James Fries published a hypothesis in the New England Journal of Medicine that still organizes most serious thinking about aging today. He called it the compression of morbidity. The idea is not that people should try to live forever. It is that the years of decline before death (what researchers call the morbidity) should be compressed into as short a window as possible, ideally right at the end, instead of stretched across a slow, decade-long taper.

Picture it as a curve with healthspan on one axis and lifespan on the other. Most people's curve starts high and drifts downward for years before it finally drops off. The goal Fries described, and the one longevity-focused medicine has organized itself around since, is to flatten that drift: to keep the curve near its ceiling for as long as possible, then let it fall quickly at the very end. Researchers sometimes call this "squaring" the curve, because the aim is a shape closer to a rectangle than a slope.

Two independent levers, not one. Raising the curve keeps more years free of disease and disability. Pushing the curve outward delays the point of terminal decline. Functional medicine, at its best, is aimed at the first lever: the one conventional care mostly leaves alone.

What actually moves the needle

If the goal is compressing morbidity rather than just chasing a bigger lifespan number, the inputs that matter shift too. A handful of measures keep showing up across the research as unusually predictive of how the second half of life actually goes.

Cardiorespiratory fitness is the strongest single one. A 2018 study of more than 122,000 patients at the Cleveland Clinic, published in JAMA Network Open, found that the mortality benefit of high cardiorespiratory fitness exceeded that of any other factor the researchers examined, including quitting smoking or controlling diabetes, and the benefit kept climbing at the highest fitness levels with no observed ceiling. Grip strength, oddly, is close behind. The Prospective Urban Rural Epidemiology study, which followed roughly 140,000 adults across seventeen countries, found that grip strength predicted death from any cause more reliably than systolic blood pressure. Muscle mass more broadly, insulin sensitivity, sleep architecture, and visceral fat all belong on the same short list.

None of this is exotic. What it is, is time-intensive to measure and even more time-intensive to act on, which is precisely the resource a fifteen-minute primary care visit rarely has. That gap in bandwidth, more than any single breakthrough compound, is the actual market opening that functional and longevity-focused medicine has grown to fill.

What a functional medicine visit is actually for

Done well, a functional medicine intake looks less like a checkup and more like an audit: extended lab panels beyond the standard annual bloodwork, a detailed history of sleep, stress, and diet, sometimes continuous glucose monitoring or VO2 max testing, and a protocol built around what the data actually shows rather than a generic supplement stack. The clinician's job is to find the specific, personal version of the healthspan curve you are already on, and to identify which lever (metabolic, muscular, hormonal, inflammatory) is doing the most damage to it.

Done poorly, it looks like a wellness retailer with a white coat: a long panel of tests you did not need, followed by a subscription of supplements you do not need either, with little of either grounded in your actual risk profile. The field has real practitioners and real theater operating under the same name, often in the same strip mall, and the interview or the intake form rarely tells you which one you have walked into.

How to tell the real thing from the theater

A few signals travel reasonably well across clinics. Real functional medicine practices order labs to answer a specific question about you, not to fill out a package tier, and they can explain in plain language why a given marker is being tracked and what a change in it would mean. Many credible practitioners hold certification through the Institute for Functional Medicine, a real and independently verifiable credential worth checking rather than taking on faith. And the strongest programs treat themselves as a complement to your existing primary and specialist care rather than a replacement for it, coordinating with your other clinicians instead of asking you to quietly walk away from them.

Pricing transparency is a smaller but useful tell. A clinic that can tell you exactly what a panel costs before you agree to it is behaving like a medical practice. A clinic that only reveals cost after a lengthy consultation, once you are emotionally invested in the plan, is behaving like a sales funnel.

The actual work

None of this collapses into a single number the way a lifespan projection can. That is the honest, slightly unsatisfying truth about healthspan: it resists the tidy headline. It is closer to a portfolio of small, compounding decisions (how you train, how you sleep, what your labs say about how your body is handling the last decade of meals) than it is to a single dial you can turn once and forget.

But the research is no longer ambiguous about the direction those decisions push. Raise the curve, and you get more years that feel like your own. Do nothing, and the curve still ends, just further from where you are standing today, with a longer, less choosable decline in between.
