If you have spent any time in the wellness corners of the internet lately, you have met the headline in one of its many costumes. "Peptides are legal again." "BPC-157 is back." "The FDA reversed itself." The claims arrive with the confidence of settled fact and the precision of a rumor. This week they got closer to true than they have ever been — and they are still wrong in a way that matters.

Start with the substances themselves. Peptides are short chains of amino acids, the same class of molecule that gives us insulin and the GLP-1 drugs. The ones at the center of this story are different: compounds like BPC-157, TB-500, KPV, and MOTS-c, used by a growing number of people for tissue repair, gut health, recovery, and the loose, expanding category of "longevity." They are popular. They are also, by and large, unproven in large human trials, which is precisely why they have been the subject of a quiet regulatory tug-of-war.

In late 2023, the FDA moved nineteen widely used peptides onto a restricted list known as Category 2, effectively barring compounding pharmacies from preparing them. The stated reason was safety: not enough human data, concerns about purity and immune reactions. Whatever the merits, the practical effect was immediate and, in hindsight, predictable. The pharmacies that had been making these peptides under medical supervision stopped. The demand did not.

Instead, buyers moved on to a grey market of "research use only" vendors with no sterility validation, no dosing standards, and no doctor anywhere in the loop.

That is the situation the current administration inherited and decided to unwind. In February 2026, Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. signaled on a podcast that he wanted these peptides made accessible again. He is, in his own words, a fan; he has said he uses them himself. What followed was not a single dramatic reversal but a slow procedural sequence — and this week it reached its loudest moment.

What the panel decided

In April 2026, the companies that had originally nominated these peptides for the restricted list withdrew their nominations, and the FDA announced that twelve of them would come off Category 2, effective 23 April 2026. That cleared the way for the second door: a review by the FDA's Pharmacy Compounding Advisory Committee, which met at the agency's White Oak campus on 23 and 24 July.

Ahead of the meeting, the FDA's own career scientists published their assessment of all seven peptides on the agenda. Their recommendation was the same in every case: do not add them to the 503A Bulks List. They cited inadequate chemical characterization, little or no human evidence of effectiveness, and unassessed safety risks including immunogenicity.

The committee went the other way on six of seven.

Thursday, 23 July. BPC-157 was recommended for the list by a vote of 8 to 6, with one abstention. Reporters in the room described an audible gasp when the tally was read — a compounding advisory panel had just voted against the agency's own written analysis. KPV and TB-500 split identically, 8–6–1. MOTS-c also cleared. A clean sweep of the day's four.

Friday, 24 July. Semax was recommended 8 to 5. Epitalon was recommended 7 to 4. Emideltide — better known as DSIP — was the only compound of the week to fail, going down 6 to 7 when one member who had voted with the majority all along crossed over.

One member who voted yes on BPC-157 described his reasoning in terms of medical freedom rather than evidence, language that industry groups quickly echoed. That framing is now part of the record, and it is worth noticing that it is a different argument from the one the committee is formally asked to make.

The votes were narrower than the headlines

Two things get lost in the "peptides are legal now" version of this story, and both are worth carrying out of the article.

The first is that the panel does not vote on a molecule in the abstract. It votes on a substance for a stated use. BPC-157 was recommended for ulcerative colitis. KPV was recommended for wound treatment and inflammatory conditions. Epitalon was recommended for insomnia; semax for migraine, cerebral ischemia, and trigeminal neuralgia. Nobody in that room voted on tendon repair, recovery, or longevity — which is, for most of the people celebrating, the entire reason they take these compounds. A listing tied to ulcerative colitis is not a blessing on the use you actually have in mind.

The second is that a recommendation is not a rule. The committee's votes are non-binding. For anything to change in practice, the FDA has to open formal notice-and-comment rulemaking on each substance and see it through — a process that takes months at best and can stretch past a year. The agency usually follows its compounding panel, but not always, and it has just been handed six recommendations that contradict its own scientists. Nothing about what you can legally buy, or what a pharmacy can legally make, changed this week.

So the honest status is: through the second door on six compounds, with the third door — actual FDA approval — not even in the building. None of these seven is an approved drug, and this week's vote did not move any of them toward that.

Why the category still matters less than the source

There is also the matter of who was doing the reviewing. Reporting on the panel's composition noted that, unlike previous compounding committees drawn largely from academic medicine, this one included several members with commercial ties to the peptide world. Reasonable people can read the result as practitioner expertise finally getting a seat at the table, or as the table being set. Both readings are in circulation, and you are allowed to hold the tension.

But here is the point that survives whichever way the rulemaking goes. The meaningful safety line for a consumer is not the regulatory category. It is the supply chain. A peptide prepared by a licensed 503A or 503B compounding pharmacy, ordered by a clinician who examined you, comes with sterility validation, known concentration, and a person who is accountable if something goes wrong. A vial from a "research use only" website comes with a disclaimer and a shrug.

Now that the vote has happened, that distinction is about to get harder to see, not easier. The grey market will read six yes votes as permission and market accordingly. Expect "FDA advisory committee approved" on checkout pages within the month, attached to products that no committee reviewed, for uses no committee considered.

The useful question to bring to this moment is still not "are peptides legal now." It is "who is the licensed clinician I would work with, and where do they source." If you are going to experiment with compounds whose evidence base is still forming, doing it with a doctor and a real pharmacy is the version of that choice that keeps the most doors open.

What happens next

The FDA now reviews the votes, the discussion, and roughly 1,860 public comments before deciding whether to initiate rulemaking on any of the six. A second committee meeting is expected before the end of February 2027 to take up five more compounds, including LL-37, GHK-Cu, Dihexa, Melanotan II, and PEG-MGF.

The real milestone was never the show of hands. It is whether, and how quickly, the FDA opens rulemaking on the six. That is the moment anything actually changes. We will update this piece when it does.
