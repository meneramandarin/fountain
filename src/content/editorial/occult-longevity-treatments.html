Every treatment you now consider ordinary spent years as a rumor. The sauna was Nordic folklore before it had cardiovascular meta-analyses. Cold plunges were a Wim Hof eccentricity before they were a backyard fixture. The vampire facial scandalized the internet in 2013; today it is offered at 36 perfectly ordinary locations on Fountain, filed between the hydrafacials and the microneedling. The frontier is not a separate world from medicine. It is medicine's waiting room, and some of what waits there eventually gets called inside.

This is a guide to the far end of that room: the strangest, the most ambitious, and the most frankly esoteric procedures you can book right now. Two things to know before we begin. Almost none of this is FDA-approved for the purpose it is sold for, and some of it is available precisely because this is a global marketplace, where other countries run their own experiments under their own rules. The evidence behind each entry ranges from a genuinely promising early trial to an open question, and we will tell you which is which. None of that makes the menu less interesting. It makes it a menu for adults.

Blood, removed and improved

The oldest intuition in medicine is that the blood carries the story of the body, and some of the newest science suggests the intuition was onto something. Therapeutic plasma exchange is an established hospital procedure: a machine separates your plasma from your blood cells, removes it along with the inflammatory proteins and autoantibodies it carries, and returns your cells suspended in clean albumin. For a list of autoimmune and neurological conditions it is standard of care. Its use in longevity is newer, and the research behind it is among the most interesting in the field.

The lineage runs through the famous parabiosis experiments, in which old mice surgically joined to young ones showed rejuvenated tissue. Two decades on, the same Berkeley lab found something more elegant: young blood was not required at all. Simply diluting old plasma with saline and albumin rejuvenated muscle, liver and brain tissue in aged mice, which suggests that aging is driven less by what old blood lacks than by what it accumulates. A first small human study reported that rounds of TPE shifted the circulating proteome measurably younger. The human data is early and the cohorts are small, but the mechanism is real science in real journals, and it is why plasma exchange has become the centerpiece of the most ambitious longevity protocols in the country.

Humanaut Health in Austin has built the fullest expression of it we have found. The Executive Blood Cleanse ($35,000) compresses two plasma exchanges, an ozone blood treatment, two hyperbaric sessions and a cellular therapy infusion with nebulized stem cells into three consecutive days, bracketed by diagnostics and followed by tailored peptide and supplement support. The unhurried version, the Complete Blood Cleanse ($75,000), unfolds over three weeks with six exchanges. In either case you are not buying a procedure so much as a supervised systemic reset: the diagnostics before, the medical team throughout, the protocol after. It is the atelier model applied to physiology, and as with any atelier, the totality is the product.

The EBO2 sessions inside those protocols deserve their own mention, because the procedure has a genuine sense of ceremony: blood drawn from one arm, filtered, enriched with ozone, exposed to ultraviolet light, and returned through the other arm visibly brighter. Ozone therapy is not FDA-approved and its clinical literature is still thin. It is also one of the most sought-after treatments on Fountain, with 179 locations offering it worldwide. Sometimes the evidence and the demand simply have not met yet.

The blue drip

Somewhere in most serious longevity clinics hangs an IV bag of arresting color. Methylene blue has one of the great biographies in pharmacology: a nineteenth-century textile dye that became the first fully synthetic drug in medicine, treated malaria for generations, and remains FDA-approved today for a rare blood disorder. Its third act is as a mitochondrial enhancer. At low doses it slots into the electron transport chain, where, the theory goes, it helps cells produce energy more efficiently. The evidence is early but not imaginary: a small randomized, placebo-controlled trial found that a single low oral dose increased activity in the brain's memory networks and modestly improved memory retrieval in healthy adults. At around $250 a session it is one of the more accessible entries in this guide, and yes, it will tint things teal for a day. Consider it proof of delivery.

It keeps interesting company on the menu. There is Poly-MVA, a proprietary complex that binds lipoic acid to palladium, and there are high-dose ozone drips, both squarely in the evidence-forming category. Chelation, the intravenous removal of metals with EDTA, has the longest paper trail of the group: a real antidote for lead poisoning, an NIH trial in 2013 that suggested a reduction in cardiovascular events, and a larger 2024 replication that did not confirm the benefit, though it did confirm the infusions lower blood lead. The science, in other words, is still arguing with itself, which is the natural condition of nearly everything in this piece.

The regenerative frontier

Stem cells are the reigning ambition of the field: 720 locations on Fountain offer them, alongside 281 offering exosomes, the cell-free signaling vesicles that many clinicians now favor for their consistency. Most of this work concerns joints, skin and hairlines. The committed end goes further. At a physician-led practice in Midtown Manhattan, protocols run from a stem cell and PRP facelift ($7,000) to full IV rejuvenation ($12,000) to image-guided injections of stem cells, PRP and exosomes delivered intrathecally, into the spinal canal ($9,000), an address otherwise reserved for hospital medicine.

None of this is FDA-approved for these uses; all of it is legal under physician discretion; and the distance between the committed end and the careless end of regenerative medicine comes down entirely to who is holding the needle. We will editorialize here to the extent of exactly one sentence: for anything in this category, choose the licensed physician in the accredited setting, every time.

The pilgrimage circuit

Longevity has always been a traveling discipline. People once crossed Europe for the waters at Baden-Baden; they now cross oceans for protocols, and the most complete esoteric menus assemble where the regulatory weather differs. In Puerto Vallarta, Holistic Bio Spa, carrying a 4.8 rating across 466 reviews, offers MUSE stem cells, plasmapheresis and full multi-week programs for chronic conditions, alongside biological dentistry, the school of thought that treats the mouth as part of systemic health. In Bangkok, Miskawaan Health Group pairs deep diagnostic packages with ozone, chelation and intermittent hypoxia-hyperoxia training: forty-five minutes in a recliner, breathing thin mountain air through a mask, then rich air, then thin again, a controlled dose of altitude as a training stimulus.

IHHT is one of the quiet overachievers of this list. A handful of small randomized trials in older adults report real, if modest, gains in cognition and mobility from these protocols. Thirty-four participants makes for a small trial, and also thirty-four more than most of this genre can claim.

What unites the far end of the menu is not any single mechanism but a posture: a willingness to act on early evidence, at your own expense, with your own clinician, before the consensus arrives. Sometimes the consensus never comes. Sometimes it arrives late; the peptides we covered in July spent three years in a regulatory no-man's-land before a federal advisory panel began letting them back in. The occult, remember, does not mean false. It means hidden: not yet examined in full light. The frontier is where the next decade's standard of care auditions, alongside a great deal that will never make the cut, and the whole pleasure and risk of being early is that you do not get to know in advance which is which.
